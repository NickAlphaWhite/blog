On December 17, 2026, Asiana Airlines is scheduled to be fully integrated into Korean Air. Founded in 1988, South Korea's second-largest airline will disappear after 38 years.

Before that happens, I decided to take one more flight. I was going from Seoul to Tokyo anyway, and OZ102 was available—one of Asiana's classic A380 routes. It would be a short two-hour trip, possibly my last chance to see this giant aircraft in Asiana colors.

---

## Background: A Five-Year Merger Marathon

In November 2020, the South Korean government announced plans to merge Asiana Airlines into Korean Air. What followed was a drawn-out series of antitrust reviews—in China, the United States, the European Union, Japan, Vietnam, Malaysia, and other markets, one by one.

On December 12, 2024, Korean Air completed its acquisition of a 63.88% stake in Asiana for 1.5 trillion won (roughly $1 billion). Asiana became a Korean Air subsidiary, with a two-year transition period.

The changes have already begun. Asiana's brand is still around, but the two airlines' identities have started to blend. You can see it especially clearly on board.

---

## Incheon Airport: Korean Air's Lounge

At Incheon's Terminal 1, Asiana business-class passengers can now use Korean Air's Prestige Class lounge. After all, they're practically part of the same family.

Korean Air's lounge is in the east wing of T1. It's spacious enough that even at peak hours it doesn't feel crowded. Dark wood tones give it a restrained look, with clearly defined seating, dining, and shower areas. Compared with Asiana's former lounges, there's less of the warmth associated with "Asiana red" and more of a businesslike atmosphere.

The Korean food selection includes bibimbap, gimbap, and Shin Ramyun—a self-service noodle station is practically standard in Korean lounges. Western options include salads, sandwiches, and soup. The drinks selection is solid as well, with Korean soju and whiskey.

A little after 8 a.m., the lounge wasn't especially busy. I sat by a window and watched Asiana and Korean Air aircraft pushing back from the apron. It was interesting to see the two airlines parked at neighboring gates: one tail carried Asiana's colorful wing, the other Korean Air's taegeuk symbol. Soon, only the latter will remain.

---

## Boarding: My First Look at OZ102

Asiana currently flies its A380s primarily from Incheon to Tokyo Narita and Los Angeles. OZ102 is a morning flight, departing around 10 a.m. and arriving in Tokyo in the afternoon.

When I reached the gate, I saw an A380 through the huge floor-to-ceiling windows—two decks, four engines, and that unmistakable Asiana tail logo. But as I got closer, I began noticing the changes.

There were three jet bridges. Business and first-class passengers on the upper deck used the one on the left; economy boarding used the other two. Asiana's ground staff checked documents individually. They were efficient but gentle, in the airline's familiar style, rather than rushing people through.

I stopped for a moment as I entered the cabin: all the tableware had been replaced with Korean Air's. The silver chopsticks and spoons carried Korean Air's taegeuk logo, not Asiana's red arrow. Yet the safety video was still Asiana's. So were the logos on the headrest covers and the red branding on the blanket packaging.

That half-Asiana, half-Korean Air feeling is what makes flying Asiana in 2026 so unusual. You're watching an airline disappear.

---

## Cabin and Seats

The upper deck of Asiana's A380 is entirely business class, with a staggered 1-2-1 layout—a variation on the Aura seat found at several other airlines. Seats alternate between positions closer to the window and the aisle, offering pretty good privacy.

Seat width and legroom are generous; that's one advantage of the A380's spacious upper deck. The in-flight entertainment screen is large enough, and the system still feels responsive for its generation. But you can tell the seats are getting old. Some adjustment buttons have lost their resistance, and the edges of the armrests show wear.

The flight was roughly 70% full. There were 23 crew members, the standard complement for this A380 service. Frequent flyers know that the size of an A380 crew is part of the experience: each flight attendant covers a relatively small section, so service feels continuous.

I'd prepared 25 small gifts for the crew. I'd read plenty of trip reports saying that Asiana and Korean Air flight attendants appreciate little gestures, and that was true today. Several crew members from the rear cabin came over to thank me. They even gave me an Asiana souvenir set—postcards and stickers featuring the airline's logo. Those may become collector's items before long.

## The Meal

ICN–NRT is only about two hours, but Asiana still serves a full meal in business class. There were three choices: Korean beef over rice, Western-style roast chicken, or a Japanese bento.

I picked the Korean beef bowl. Ordering Korean food on a Korean airline seems like the obvious move.

The beef bowl was genuinely good. Thin slices of beef had been simmered in a flavorful Korean sauce, and the seasoning worked perfectly with the steamed rice. The rice itself was soft and slightly sticky, as you'd expect in Korea. Getting rice of that quality on an airplane isn't easy.

The kimchi, though, was another story. In-flight kimchi simply can't compare with the version on the ground. The fermented smell was overpowering, the cabbage had lost its crunch, and it sat on the tray like an uninvited guest. I gave up after one bite. Serving kimchi on a plane is an adventure in itself—it's a bit of an olfactory weapon. There's something funny about opening a packet of it while the person next to you is sipping red wine.

Dessert was a yuzu mousse, with just the right balance of sweet and tart. A refreshing finish.

The cutlery was definitely new. Korean Air's taegeuk logo was embossed on the silver chopsticks and spoon, which felt substantial in the hand. The white tablecloth was embossed as well, without a trace of Asiana branding. Tableware must have been among the first things they changed. It's easy enough to swap a logo.

Yet the little condiment bottle beside the plate still belonged to Asiana. Nobody must have bothered replacing it. It had become a tiny relic.

---

## The Flight Itself

Anyone who's flown an A380 knows just how quiet it is.

When this four-engine giant took off from Incheon, there was plenty of thrust but almost no engine noise in the cabin. At cruising altitude, it was unbelievably quiet. All I could hear was the white noise of the ventilation and my own breathing. It creates a strange sense of unreality: you're traveling through the troposphere at about 900 kilometers per hour inside a metal machine weighing nearly 600 tons, but it feels like sitting in a library.

The ride was smooth, with almost no turbulence. That's hardly surprising. The A380's sheer size makes it an excellent shock absorber.

I definitely felt the landing, though. That's another A380 characteristic: when the main landing gear's 20 wheels meet the runway, you know you've arrived. It's not like a 787 landing that can be so gentle you wonder whether you're still in the air. This wasn't a hard landing, just a solid one—as if the pilots wanted to give this old friend a fitting goodbye.

---

## Safety Video and Small Details

The safety video was still pure Asiana. Flight attendants in Asiana uniforms demonstrated seat belts and oxygen masks against a backdrop of Seoul landmarks. It hadn't been replaced, and perhaps nobody was in a hurry to do so. The uniforms, however, already showed subtle changes. Name badges now included the words "Member of Korean Air Group."

The gray blankets had been in service for years, but they still bore the Asiana logo. So did the red headrest covers. There were no amenity kits on this short flight, but the slippers were still Asiana-branded.

I caught myself unconsciously "looking for Asiana"—checking where the red arrow remained and where the taegeuk had replaced it. It was a peculiar experience. I was on an Asiana Airlines A380 that no longer felt entirely Asiana.

---

## Farewell to Asiana

Asiana Airlines was founded in 1988, the year Seoul hosted the Olympics. Over 38 years, it grew from a regional carrier into one of the Asia-Pacific's most important airlines, known for its service quality and safety record. It joined Star Alliance in 2012 and introduced the A380 in 2014, becoming one half of South Korea's great airline rivalry.

Then came the pandemic in 2020 and the financial crisis that changed everything.

After December 17, 2026, Asiana's name is expected to disappear from the IATA registry. Its six A380s are slated to leave service gradually before 2030 as the fleets are integrated.

It isn't entirely a sad story. A unified Korean flag carrier will be better positioned to compete internationally. But aviation enthusiasts understand: once a livery, a tail logo, or the soundtrack to a safety video disappears, it may be gone forever.

OZ102 landed at Narita on time. As we taxied in, I spotted an ANA A380 nearby, wearing its blue sea turtle livery with layers of green, blue, and orange. Among the A380 giants I think of—Singapore Airlines, Asiana, and ANA—ANA's distinctive fleet would remain.

As I stepped out, I looked back toward the cockpit. The door was open, and the captain was organizing his flight log. I didn't say goodbye, but I knew I might never see this scene again.

*Farewell, Asiana.*
